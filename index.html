Whose Queensland Is in the Archive?

The State Library of Queensland has put tens of thousands of old photographs online. They are a window on the past, but a window has a frame. Who and what did the camera pick out, and who was left outside it?

52,118photographs in the library’s Picture Queensland collection file
9 in 10are black and white; only 142 are in colour
1 in 3has no recorded location, so it can’t appear on any map

A note on what is missing: this file is labelled as non-Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander material. Collections like this are kept separate from First Nations material so that it can be handled under its own cultural protocols. This page therefore shows Queensland as seen through the photographs in this part of the archive, and it is not a complete record of the state’s people.

1 · Meet the archive

A Queensland of the early twentieth century

Almost all of the photographs that carry both a date and a place were taken between the 1860s and the 1950s. The collection builds to a peak in the 1920s, then falls away sharply near the 1950s. Most of these images are anonymous: fewer than one in ten names the person who took the photograph.

2 · Where

The camera kept returning to the south-east

We look at the distribution of images taken in Queensland over time. Each circle below is a town or suburb; its area shows how many photographs were catalogued there. Place names are matched to the centre of the town, not the exact spot where the picture was taken.

Nearly four in ten photographs that have a location are in the City of Brisbane. Beyond the capital, a chain of coastal towns stands out, and so do Toowoomba and Warwick inland.

3 · Photographs versus people

Who lives where, and who got photographed

Nearly 38% of the photographs with a location are in the City of Brisbane, which is home to about 24% of Queenslanders today. Look at the areas around it: the Gold Coast, Moreton Bay and Logan together are home to about 29% of Queenslanders but hold less than 4% of the photographs.

Brisbane is first in every era. Behind it the order changes: only five council areas stay in the top twelve for photographs in every era.

4 · What was photographed

What the camera pointed at

Captions give a rough idea of the subject. The chart below counts how often each theme appears in the photos of each decade. One photograph can touch several themes, so the shares do not add to 100%. Themes come from simple keyword matching and are a guide only. Buildings and streets lead in every decade, but war and the military jump to about a third of the photos in the 1940s.

Buildings and streets remain the most photographed theme in every decade, followed by landscape, farming and transport. A notable jump comes in the 1940s: with the onslaught of the Second World War, the share of images with a war context rises to 32%.

Different towns were photographed at different times. Some places, such as Mount Isa, have sharp bursts of photographs; others, such as Brisbane, are spread across the whole period.

Method notes

  • Dates were read from the catalogue’s date field; where it was blank, a decade stated in the subject field was used.
  • Places were located using the coordinates in the catalogue record, which point to the town or suburb centre.
  • Themes are keyword matches on titles, descriptions and subjects.
